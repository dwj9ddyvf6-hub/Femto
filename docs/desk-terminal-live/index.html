How a setup earns its place

No single signal is trusted on its own. A stock becomes a setup here when several independent ways of reading it point the same way at the same time — the desk reads the whole picture at once, never one indicator at a time. Traders call that kind of whole-pattern read a gestalt: the pattern means more than any of its parts. Every setup is scored out of 100 across the lenses below, and every point is explained on its brief — nothing is a black box.

  • Independent agreement. The desk runs dozens of strategies built on different ideas. When strategies that share nothing — different signals, different mathematics — select the same stock on the same day, that agreement is the strongest evidence the desk knows. Close cousins count once, so one family cannot stuff the ballot box.
  • The pattern engine. A dedicated engine grades swing patterns: the shapes prices make as they base, pull back and turn. A fresh, confirmed pattern adds serious weight. No pattern, no points from this lens.
  • The chart-review board. An independent nightly ranking of chart quality across a fixed board of stocks. It is background, never a trigger: it can support a setup the other lenses found, but it can never create one by itself.
  • The market’s mood. The same setup means different things in different markets. Washed-out dips are at their best in fearful, falling markets; momentum needs a rising one. The desk reads the broad market first and scores the fit.
  • A price sitting at a level. Setups score higher when the price has pulled back and reset onto something real — its recent average, or the zone of its recent lows — instead of dangling mid-range.
  • The shape of the risk. A good setup knows where it is wrong. A defined exit level, far enough away that ordinary noise will not trigger it but close enough to cap the loss, earns points; a stop so tight that a normal day would shake it out loses them.
  • The warning signs. Some things count against a setup no matter how many lenses agree: a price that has already spiked hard in a few sessions, one stretched far above its average, or one that has broken below its recent lows. Each of those deductions exists because that exact pattern has burned traders before.

The brief above walks the lenses for this one stock: the chart as it looked when the setup fired, the evidence lens by lens, and the list of what would end the setup. The list itself is a research starting point — the evidence is published with every setup so you can check the desk’s work and decide for yourself.

The mix, in pictures

Words we use

  • Option: a contract giving the right to buy or sell something at an agreed price, by an agreed date.
  • Call: an option that gains in value when the price rises.
  • Put: an option that gains in value when the price falls.
  • Strike: the agreed price at which an option lets you buy or sell.
  • Premium: simply the price paid for an option — or the payment collected when one is sold.
  • Spread: a paired option trade — one option bought and one sold on the same stock — so the most that can be made or lost is capped.
  • Contract: one option contract covers 100 shares.
  • Long: owned — the desk bought it and gains when the price rises.
  • Short: sold first, as a bet the price will fall, and bought back later.
  • Expiry: the date an option runs out.
  • Straddle: buying a call and a put together on the same stock.
  • Wheel: the repeating cycle of selling a put, possibly buying the shares, then selling calls against them.
  • Ladder: options stacked at different prices and dates, like rungs of a ladder.
  • Beta: a measure of how much a stock moves compared with the whole market; high-beta stocks move more.
  • Bar: one period's price summary — where the price opened, its high, its low, and where it closed.
  • ETF: a fund traded like a stock, holding a basket of shares or assets.
  • Tensor: the desk's name for its pattern-scoring engine, which grades stocks for swing setups.
  • VWAP: the day's average traded price, weighted by how much traded at each price.
